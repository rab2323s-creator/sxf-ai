Amazon HOPE explained: can 50% MoE pruning preserve coding accuracy?

SOURCEAmazon Research / Hugging Face
PUBLISHEDNovember 9, 2026
LAYERTools
SIGNAL SCORE28/100

SXF VERIFIED BRIEF

Amazon introduced HOPE, a method for pruning mixture-of-experts models that measures pairwise expert co-activation rather than ranking every expert independently. The team shared a paper, open code and a calibration–solve–prune workflow.

WHY IT MATTERS

Large MoE models keep many expert weights in memory even when each token activates only a fraction. Cutting model weights may let developers run longer contexts or reduce hardware requirements, but inaccurate pruning can damage reasoning and coding behavior.

WHAT TO VERIFY

The 50% figure is the fraction of experts pruned, not a proven 50% reduction in overall server cost, latency or power use. The coding benchmark numbers were produced under the authors' evaluation settings, and HOPE's advantage is smaller at light pruning levels.

TECHNICAL ANALYSIS / 01

Why mixture-of-experts models consume memory even when experts sit idle

A mixture-of-experts (MoE) model routes each input token to a small subset of specialized feed-forward networks. This lowers active computation compared with executing every expert, but the server still typically holds the expert parameters in memory. Amazon's example is Qwen3.5-122B: its report describes 256 experts in each of 48 layers, with eight used for a given token, while expert MLPs account for most of the parameter footprint.

Deleting unused or less useful experts can permanently shrink a model without training it again. Yet the obvious technique—score each expert individually and remove the lowest-ranked ones—assumes the expert's contribution is independent of its partners. That assumption can fail. Two experts frequently selected together might cooperate to solve a task, and eliminating one changes the function of both.

The search question 'Can I cut a 122B MoE model in half?' needs a careful answer: pruning half the experts is not the same as making total hardware needs or running expenses exactly half as large. Memory outside the expert weights, request-specific cache and runtime overhead still matter.

TECHNICAL ANALYSIS / 02

How HOPE selects experts and what the 50% benchmark actually shows

HOPE uses calibration data to record how experts are activated and how their outputs combine. It builds a matrix whose diagonal records individual contributions while its off-diagonal terms represent pairwise co-operation. Instead of deleting experts one by one, the system approximates a joint selection problem and solves a relaxed quadratic optimization before writing a new checkpoint.

The researchers compare HOPE with REAP, MAN, EAN and Frequency pruning on three model families across two calibration datasets and multiple pruning rates. They report the best average ranking for HOPE overall and a stronger advantage when removing around half of experts. The reported 50% pruning mean rank is 1.58 versus 2.42 for REAP.

One highlighted example uses Qwen3.5-35B-A3B on SWE-Bench Pro. HOPE scored 42.3 versus 41.8 for the unpruned model, while REAP reached 36.2 in that setup. This does not prove that pruning improves every model; retained score can depend heavily on which tasks appear in calibration and evaluation. The authors themselves note reduced advantage at 10–20% pruning.

TECHNICAL ANALYSIS / 03

A deployment checklist: benchmark quality, VRAM and total cost

For an engineer considering HOPE, begin with a baseline checkpoint, a representative calibration set and a held-out test that reflects the actual production task. Run the published calibrate, solve and prune workflow, then measure factual accuracy, agent task success, first-token latency, tokens per second, memory high-water mark and cost under identical serving conditions.

Do not assume a promising coding benchmark means the same performance on multilingual instruction following, safety-relevant answers or tool-use policies. Measure reliability at realistic context lengths and compare against both the original checkpoint and simpler pruning methods. Independent repeatability matters more than a headline that a 50% model is 'just as smart.'

HOPE's practical contribution is a more informed selection of which experts to keep. Its code and research paper make that hypothesis testable, while the limitations—pairwise approximation, independent layer pruning and architecture-specific behavior—make it a research tool rather than a universal model-shrinking guarantee.

Release and research

Memory example

The authors cite Qwen3.5-122B with 256 experts in each of 48 layers, eight experts selected per token and around 95% of an approximately 250 GB parameter footprint in expert MLPs. Primary evidence ↗

Method

HOPE scores pairwise cooperation with a calibrated interaction matrix, then uses quadratic optimization to choose experts to remove. Primary evidence ↗

Comparison design

The evaluation covers three models, two calibration datasets, four first-order baselines and 10–50% pruning rates. Primary evidence ↗

Overall ranking

HOPE's reported mean rank is 2.07 among five methods, finishing first in 39% and top two in 67% of evaluated conditions. Primary evidence ↗

High pruning

At 50% pruning its mean rank is 1.58, versus 2.42 for REAP, the reported next-best method. Primary evidence ↗

Coding example

On Qwen3.5-35B-A3B at 50% pruning, SWE-Bench Pro scores are reported as HOPE 42.3, unpruned 41.8 and REAP 36.2. Primary evidence ↗

Implementation

The open package documents calibration, a QP solve and writing a pruned checkpoint; it has been tested on Qwen3, Qwen3-Next, Qwen3.5 and GLM-4.5. Primary evidence ↗

Research boundary

The authors say lower 10–20% pruning levels offer smaller gains and cross-layer pruning remains preliminary. Primary evidence ↗

LIMITATIONS

HOPE is evaluated on a specified set of MoE architectures, tasks and calibration traces. A 50% expert count reduction does not automatically imply the same reduction in total VRAM, electricity, inference latency or ownership cost, since attention weights, KV cache and serving overhead remain. Pairwise interactions omit higher-order and cross-layer effects, and the paper's benchmark improvements are not independently verified for every workload.

PRACTICAL TAKEAWAYS

What readers can check next.

Verification checklist

  1. Check actual VRAM and KV-cache usage, not just fraction of experts removed.
  2. Use held-out benchmarks beyond the calibration data and intended workload.
  3. Compare latency, throughput and quality under identical hardware settings.
  4. Read the released research and license before using a pruned model commercially.
