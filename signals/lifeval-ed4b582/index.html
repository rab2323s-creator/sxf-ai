OpenAI LifeVal benchmark: can AI complete everyday tasks?

SOURCEOpenAI
PUBLISHEDNovember 2, 2026
LAYERTools
SIGNAL SCORE28/100

SXF VERIFIED BRIEF

OpenAI published LifeVal, an evaluation focused on the everyday jobs people want an AI assistant to handle. It draws on existing time-use studies and a representative survey of 3,995 respondents, then tests scenarios that reach beyond answering general-knowledge questions.

WHY IT MATTERS

A model that explains a government form is not necessarily able to submit it, verify a payment or secure a childcare place. LifeVal moves the evaluation conversation toward outcomes people care about, making it more relevant to consumers, product designers and researchers than another abstract leaderboard alone.

WHAT TO VERIFY

The announcement offers example task percentages, but these figures belong to OpenAI's evaluation setup. Examine the rubrics, task constraints, permitted tools and whether an action was completed or left awaiting a person's approval before treating a score as practical reliability.

TECHNICAL ANALYSIS / 01

What LifeVal actually measures — and what it does not

Traditional AI leaderboards often ask whether a system can solve a test or answer an expert question. LifeVal instead begins with activities people spend time doing: comparing schools, coordinating a family decision, planning shopping or navigating public services. OpenAI used time-use datasets and a survey of 3,995 people to shape the task mix. That is a significant choice of measurement target: practical assistance becomes the object of evaluation, not simply factual recall.

There is a crucial boundary between producing a plausible plan and producing a verified result. A childcare shortlist may be useful even though the parent must confirm eligibility and submit an application. A budget worksheet may be accurate but still rely on bank figures that the assistant cannot verify. SXF therefore treats the published task percentages as performance under particular conditions, not as probabilities that a user-facing assistant will finish any matching real-world job.

A responsible comparison should report the exact requested outcome, the tools allowed, what the model was permitted to change and how the evaluator confirmed success. Without these details, two superficially similar percentages may capture quite different levels of autonomy.

TECHNICAL ANALYSIS / 02

Three levels of usefulness: explain, prepare, act

For a reader choosing an AI assistant, the decisive question is which layer of a task is being completed. Explaining options is useful but mostly informational. Preparing a draft, shortlist or form is more operational. Actually acting in an external service introduces new questions: consent, authentication, irreversibility and a reliable record of what changed. The LifeVal examples themselves illustrate why these layers should not be collapsed into a single headline.

Consider a request to find a school after moving. A helpful system can identify nearby options and organize application requirements; it must still check that deadlines, catchment rules and availability remain current. For health-related meal planning, suggestions are not a substitute for qualified medical advice or confirmation of medication and dietary restrictions. Humans should review consequential decisions and make the final call.

SXF's practical reading: rate an assistant along four separate dimensions — factual accuracy, completeness of the preparation, verifiable completion and permission discipline. High performance on one dimension does not automatically establish the others.

TECHNICAL ANALYSIS / 03

How users and product teams can test the promise

Start with a repeatable task that has a clear finish line: organize a set of bills, summarize school choices, or compare three public-service forms. Give the assistant only the data and permissions needed. Record which steps it did independently, which remained drafts, and which required corrections. Do not count a persuasive explanation as a completed external transaction.

Product teams should supplement LifeVal-style benchmarks with exception cases: outdated forms, ambiguous names, inaccessible websites, missing prices and a user's last-minute change of instructions. Track recovery rates and the frequency of false claims of completion. An agent is not dependable merely because it can navigate a happy-path demonstration.

What makes this news consequential is the measurement direction. If future evaluations consistently disclose tool access and externally verified outcomes, comparisons between personal assistants could become more meaningful to ordinary users. For now, LifeVal is a useful research framework and evidence of provider priorities, not a guarantee of autonomous everyday service.

What the evidence establishes.

Research population

OpenAI says its task selection combines two time-use research sources with a demographically representative survey of 3,995 respondents. Primary evidence ↗

Tasks covered

The evaluation spans caregiving, health, education, household administration, personal finances, shopping and access to government services. Primary evidence ↗

Childcare example

Public-safety example

A flood-volunteer coordination scenario displays 98.0% in the published examples, based on OpenAI's own scoring. Primary evidence ↗

Action versus advice

OpenAI presents LifeVal as a way to track assistance in concrete workflows, not as an independent measurement that every task can be executed autonomously. Primary evidence ↗

Model progress claim

OpenAI attributes improving practical workflows to gains in browser, computer-use and connector abilities; no universal consumer success rate is established. Primary evidence ↗

LIMITATIONS

LifeVal results shown in a provider publication are not independent field trials. Example percentages depend on evaluation rubrics, tool access and task conditions; they do not demonstrate that childcare enrolment, financial transfers or health decisions are safely completed without human supervision.

PRACTICAL TAKEAWAYS

What readers can check next.

Verification checklist

  1. Define a task outcome that can be independently confirmed, not merely described.
  2. Give the assistant explicit tool permissions and a human approval point before irreversible actions.
  3. Test with stale information and incomplete records, then record correction and recovery rates.
  4. Keep benchmark percentages separate from personal success guarantees.
