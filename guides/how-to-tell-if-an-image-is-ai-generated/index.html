The important question is not “Does it look like AI?”
It is “What evidence tells us how it was made—and what it really shows?”

THE DIRECT ANSWER

How can you tell if an image is AI-generated?

First, reverse-search the image with Google Lens. Then check the original file for Content Credentials (C2PA) and use the relevant official watermark tool—Gemini/SynthID for supported Google content or OpenAI Verify for supported OpenAI content. Finally, verify the publisher and caption. Missing metadata, clean-looking hands or a “human” detector score do not prove a photo is real. It may remain unverified. Use the free SXF decision guide.

Can you actually detect an AI-generated image in 2026?

Sometimes—but there is no universal, foolproof “AI image detector” for every image from every model.

Modern AI images can have correct hands, natural light and flawless text. Meanwhile, real photos can look uncanny after sharpening, low-light processing, filters or compression. Visual tells help you notice an inconsistency; they do not establish origin. Even a seemingly perfect image could be synthetic.

The strongest available clues concern provenance: a traceable publisher, a cryptographically signed creation record, or an officially detectable watermark. Each proves something different. A signed file history does not prove that an event happened, and an image with no detectable watermark could still be AI-made. Google and OpenAI describe exactly these limits in their own verification guidance.

Provenance answers: “How was this file made?”

Signed records and official watermarks may identify tools or creation steps for supported images.

Corroboration answers: “Did this happen?”

Original reporting, reliable eyewitness evidence and context establish whether the caption matches reality.

2026 update: Google announced worldwide English-language access to its SynthID Detector on October 7, 2026. Supported media and partners are expanding, but a negative check still does not certify that an image is human-created.

A PRACTICAL 2–5 MINUTE WORKFLOW

7 free ways to check if a photo was made with AI

Do the first three checks before interpreting visual details. For the most reliable answer, obtain the original file where possible—not just a cropped screenshot sent in a chat.

01

Reverse-search the image

Use Google Lens to find earlier copies, the earliest known publisher and whether the caption changed. Open matching pages rather than trusting thumbnails.

Best for: viral posts, old photos recycled as breaking news.Open Google Lens ↗
02

Use Google’s About this image

Look for first-indexing context, other sites using the image, available metadata and supported provenance signals. Access varies by platform and region.

Best for: context, not a universal AI yes/no verdict.Google’s official walkthrough ↗
03

Inspect signed Content Credentials

If you have the original file, upload it to the official Content Credentials verifier. Review the signed issuer and edit history, not just whether any metadata exists.

Best for: files with C2PA provenance; missing credentials prove nothing.Open Content Credentials Verify ↗
04

Check for an OpenAI provenance signal

For images suspected to come from ChatGPT or OpenAI tools, use OpenAI Verify. It checks supported signals such as C2PA and SynthID, not every AI generator.

Best for: identifying supported OpenAI-origin signals.Open OpenAI Verify ↗
05

Check Google AI watermark signals

In Gemini, upload a supported image and ask whether it was generated or edited by Google AI. In October 2026, Google also expanded the SynthID Detector globally in English. Product coverage is not universal.

Best for: supported Google AI and partner watermark workflows; check tool-specific limitations.Read Gemini’s verification instructions ↗
06

Inspect the original file and visual details

A camera model or creation date in EXIF can offer clues, but ordinary metadata can be altered or stripped. Look at reflections, repeated textures, tiny text and anatomy—then corroborate elsewhere.

Best for: finding questions to investigate, not issuing a verdict.Why file signals can disappear ↗
07

Verify the event, not just the pixels

If the image claims a real disaster, celebrity endorsement or breaking event, check whether a credible first-hand witness, local agency, newsroom or independent reporting supports it. If someone asks for money, stop until you verify independently.

Best for: high-stakes claims, scams and misleading captions.How image context helps ↗

Three everyday examples: how the checks change your decision

These are illustrative walkthroughs, not measured detector test results. Each shows how to reason from evidence, rather than claim a tool returned a result we never tested.

Scenario 1 · A “breaking news” disaster photo in a group chat

You have only a screenshot. A reverse-image search finds the same scene published years before the alleged event. That is meaningful evidence of a misleading caption or date—even if the photograph itself is real. Ask an authoritative local source whether the event occurred before sharing or donating.

Scenario 2 · A flawless profile portrait

No extra fingers, no warped glasses and a detector says “probably human.” None of those establishes identity. If the profile asks for money or personal documents, verify the person independently and do not use the image as sole identity proof.

Scenario 3 · A file exported from an AI generator

A verified C2PA record explicitly identifies an AI-generating tool or an official watermark check detects a supported AI signal. You now have positive origin evidence for that specific file. You still need context to know who posted it, why, and whether a related factual claim is true.

Five mistakes that lead people to falsely label a photo

  1. “The hands look normal, so it must be real.” Modern generators often render hands well; cameras also create distortions.
  2. “The detector says 99%, so the case is closed.” A classifier estimate is not a validated provenance record.
  3. “I cannot find it on Google, so it is AI.” New, private or unindexed photos have no web footprint.
  4. “There is no metadata, therefore a human took it.” Social apps and screenshots routinely remove it.
  5. “A signed camera photo proves the claim happened.” A real camera can photograph a staged scene, altered print or generated image on a screen.
Before sharing a sensitive claim: Ask three questions: Who first published this? What independent source corroborates the scene? What would change my mind? If none can be answered, label it unverified—not definitely fake or definitely real.

FAQs: Is this image AI-generated?

How can I tell if a photo is AI-generated?

Reverse-search it, trace the source, check signed provenance where possible and use relevant official watermark tools. No single absent clue proves authenticity.

Is there a free AI image detector online?

Yes, free or free-access verification routes include Content Credentials Verify, Google’s supported verification features and OpenAI Verify. Some require an account or limit usage. These services check specific signals—not all possible generators.

Can Google Lens detect AI photos?

Lens primarily searches for image matches. Google’s About this image may surface context, metadata and supported SynthID signals; absence of such data is inconclusive.

Does missing C2PA metadata mean an image is authentic?

No. Credentials may be stripped by screenshots, recompression, format conversions or social sharing.

How can I check an image made by ChatGPT?

Try OpenAI Verify with the original image. It looks for supported OpenAI provenance signals, but not-detected does not prove human origin.

Are AI image detector percentages accurate?

They are classifier outputs whose reliability depends on data, model and transformations. Treat an isolated percentage as a prompt to check other evidence, not a verdict.

Can I check an image from WhatsApp or Instagram?

You can reverse-search it and verify the claimed event. If it is a compressed forwarded screenshot, original credentials may be missing; request the original file before making a definitive provenance claim.
